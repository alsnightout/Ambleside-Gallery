Fred Stein

THE ARTIST

Humanity
in the
telling moment.

Fred Stein was born in Dresden, Germany, on July 3, 1909. As a teenager, he became deeply interested in politics and was an early opponent of the Nazi movement.

Stein studied law at Leipzig University, driven by strong humanist ideals. Although he earned his law degree in a remarkably short period of time, the Nazi government denied him admission to the German bar for what it described as “racial and political reasons.”

As the political situation became increasingly dangerous and the SS began making inquiries about him, Stein fled Germany in 1933 with his new wife, Liselotte Salzburg. Under the pretext of taking a honeymoon, the couple escaped to Paris.

There, surrounded by expatriates, intellectuals and artists, Stein began photographing seriously. Using the Leica camera he and his wife had purchased as a wedding present, he entered the streets of Paris and began documenting the lives unfolding around him.

In circumstances marked by political upheaval, approaching war and financial hardship, Stein's photographs nevertheless searched for humanity. He found moments of beauty, dignity and hope in places where others might have seen only despair.

A MAN OF VISION

“He truly was a man of vision, and his choice of people and subjects is the obvious proof of it.”

— Willy Brandt, Chancellor of the Federal Republic of Germany, 1983

PARIS · 1933–1941

A Leica
in the
streets.

Stein became a pioneer of the small, hand-held camera. The Leica gave him a freedom of movement particularly suited to the way he wanted to see the world: spontaneously, intimately and without elaborate intervention.

He photographed ordinary scenes of Parisian life alongside portraits of prominent European intellectuals and cultural figures. His camera became a means of observing humanity during one of Europe's most turbulent periods.

When Germany declared war on France in 1939, Stein was interned as an enemy alien near Paris. He eventually escaped and made a dangerous clandestine journey through the French countryside.

Stein reunited with his wife and young daughter in Marseilles. With visas obtained through the efforts of the International Rescue Committee, the family boarded the S.S. Winnipeg on May 7, 1941.

They left France carrying little more than Stein's Leica and a collection of negatives.

NEW YORK

Fifth Avenue
to
Harlem.

New York offered Stein another extraordinary landscape of human life. He walked throughout the city, photographing its streets from Fifth Avenue to Harlem.

At the same time, he photographed writers, artists, scientists, politicians and philosophers whose work he often already knew through his extensive reading. That knowledge allowed him to engage his subjects in conversation while photographing them.

Stein worked quickly and unobtrusively. He preferred natural or minimal lighting, avoided elaborate photographic setups and rejected dramatic effects for their own sake.

He did not retouch or manipulate his negatives. Instead, he valued the freedom of photography to capture the revealing instant — allowing the subject to emerge in its own light.

THE PHOTOGRAPH

A Humanist Eye

CAMERA

An early practitioner of small, hand-held Leica photography.

LIGHT

Preferred natural or minimal lighting rather than elaborate studio setups.

PROCESS

Avoided retouching and manipulation of the photographic negative.

STREET

Photographed everyday human life in Paris and New York with an unobtrusive, spontaneous approach.

PORTRAIT

Photographed many of the writers, artists, scientists, politicians and philosophers who shaped twentieth-century intellectual and cultural life.

VALUES

His photography was informed by a deep commitment to social equality and concern for his fellow human beings.

LEGACY

The Life Behind the Image

PHOTO LEAGUE

Stein became a member of the Photo League, reflecting his commitment to socially engaged photography and human equality.

REPORTAGE

His portraits and reportage appeared in newspapers, magazines and books throughout the world.

EXHIBITIONS

During his lifetime, Stein presented numerous one-man exhibitions and frequently lectured on photography.

BOOKS

Several books devoted to Stein's photography were published during his lifetime.

1967

Fred Stein died in 1967 at the age of fifty-eight.

AN ICONIC PORTRAIT

Albert
Einstein.

Among Stein's many portraits, his photograph of Albert Einstein became his most famous image.

It is an image particularly suited to Stein's philosophy of portraiture: direct, restrained and concerned less with photographic spectacle than with the humanity of the person before the camera.

Across both portraiture and street photography, Stein's work reflects the same fundamental concern — the individual human being and the fleeting moment in which character reveals itself.
